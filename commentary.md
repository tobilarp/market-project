## 2 November 2026: three weeks at once

I haven't written a note since 6 October so this one covers everything up to Friday 30 October in one go. Looking at three weeks together actually helped, because the biggest thing going on only really shows when you add the days up.

Money has been moving out of tech. Since 6 October the US tech fund (XLK) is down 11.1% and the Nasdaq 100 fund (QQQ) is down 9.1%. Over the same time the US healthcare fund (XLV) is up 10.6% and my screen flagged it twice, on 14 October (up 2.53%) and 29 October (up 2.40%). It's the same thing I saw on 17 September with money leaving risky tech and going somewhere safer. This time it's just a lot bigger.

Friday 30 October was my busiest day so far with five flags. XLK fell 4.89%, QQQ fell 3.47%, the S&P 500 fund (SPY) fell 1.77%, gold (GLD) fell 3.47% and silver (SLV) fell 5.67%. Five flags doesn't mean five things happened though. XLK, QQQ and SPY all hold the same big tech companies so one tech selloff got counted three times. Gold and silver normally move together so that's one metals move counted twice. So really it was two events. That's the thing I most want to fix, because my screen checks every fund on its own and can't tell when a few flags are actually the same story.

Metals are the part that doesn't fit what I said last time. Since 6 October silver is down 19.6% and gold is down 6.9%. Back then I put gold falling down to interest rates going up and the dollar getting stronger. But since then long term US government bonds (TLT) are up 5.9%, which means rates have actually been coming down and the dollar fund (UUP) has barely moved at 0.3%. So my explanation worked for September but it doesn't work for October. Something else is dragging metals down and my data can't tell me what yet.

Oil is still going the other way. Since I started tracking on 27 August, Brent crude (BNO) is up 38.1% and US crude (USO) is up 29.9%. Both are the highest they've been on my dashboard. Oil and gas companies (XLE) are up 12.4%. The gap between Brent and US crude is still about 8 points, which is pretty much where it was on 6 October. I said before that the gap should close once shipping calms down. It hasn't closed, which fits with my guess, but it doesn't prove it.

Next I want to see if healthcare keeps winning if tech starts to recover, or if they just swap back.

## 6 October 2026 — answering my own gold question

On 9 September I wrote that I didn't know why gold was falling during a
geopolitical shock, when it's meant to be the safe place money runs to.
Four weeks later, my data has given me an answer.

Since 9 September, gold (GLD) is down 5.9% and silver (SLV) is down 9.2%.
On 28 September both were flagged by my screen on the same day: gold
−3.94% and silver −5.49%. Over the same period, long-dated US government
bonds (TLT) fell 5.7% and the dollar (UUP) rose 3.6%.

Those three moves fit together. Falling bond prices mean rising interest
rates, and the Federal Reserve raised rates on 16 September. Gold pays no
interest, so when you can earn more just holding bonds or cash, holding
gold costs you more. A stronger dollar adds to that, because gold is
priced in dollars, so it gets more expensive for everyone else. On 9
September I said this explanation pointed the right way but looked far too
weak. Over four weeks, it has held up better than anything else I've
tested.

I should be careful, though. These moves happening together doesn't prove
one caused the other, and with daily closing prices I can't see which
moved first.

The other thing I'm watching is the two oil prices. Since tracking began
on 27 August, Brent crude (BNO), the global benchmark, is up 18.8%, but US
crude (USO) is up only 10.8%. On 22 September they were only about a point
apart. My guess is that disruption around the Strait of Hormuz hits oil
shipped by sea, which is priced off Brent, harder than oil produced inside
the US. My data can't confirm that yet. If the gap closes once shipping
calms down, the guess was probably right.

## 17 September 2026 — my screen's first real flags

On Monday 14 September my dashboard flagged its first unusual moves. EEM
(an emerging markets fund) fell 2.73%, and XLV (a US healthcare fund) rose
1.45%. They looked like two separate stories. I think they were one.

EEM isn't really a bet on "emerging markets" in general. Over 40% of it is
technology companies, and nearly half is Taiwan and South Korea, which are
home to the world's biggest chipmakers. So the flag was a chip selloff.
Healthcare rising on the same day was the other side of it: money leaving
risky tech and going somewhere safer.

The trigger was the chief executives of Anthropic and OpenAI calling for
the industry to slow down AI development. If AI companies build more
slowly, they buy fewer chips, so chipmakers expect to sell fewer at lower
prices. Companies that *buy* chips benefit from the same news, because the
money chipmakers lose is money buyers no longer have to spend. The news
moved them in opposite directions.

My first reaction was that the AI bubble was bursting. The next two days
don't support that. EEM fell another 0.35% and then 0.06%, so it didn't
crash further, but it didn't bounce either. Healthcare kept its gain. My
reading is that investors aren't panicking, but they aren't convinced it's
over.

That reading has a complication. On Wednesday the Federal Reserve raised
interest rates for the first time since 2023, to 3.75–4.00%, and higher
rates also tend to push tech stocks down. So was it the AI news or the
Fed? The currencies suggest the Fed wasn't the main reason. The dollar had
its biggest rise of the week that day (UUP, the US dollar fund, +0.64%),
so the rate rise clearly landed. But US tech (XLK) actually rose 0.10%,
and EEM barely moved. If the Fed were holding chip stocks down, they
should have fallen on the day it acted.

What would settle it: if chip stocks keep drifting lower next week with no
new AI news, the market believes the slowdown is real; if they recover,
Monday was an overreaction. The bigger test is whether AI companies
actually cut what they spend on chips. Until they do, this is a fear, not
a fact.

## 9 September 2026 — marking my own homework

On 1 September I argued the oil move was a risk premium on supply that
hadn't been lost yet, and that higher crude should raise what the market
expects energy companies to earn. Four sessions later, here's what my own
data did to that.

The premium held. Crude is up 3.57% from the 1 September close, including
another 2.87% on Monday. Nothing has stopped flowing, and the price is
still carrying the risk.

It also stayed specific to crude. Natural gas fell 1.13% over the same
stretch — so this wasn't energy repricing, it was oil repricing.

The part I got wrong was the second half. Energy equities closed at 64.77
on 1 September and at 64.77 on 8 September — exactly flat, while the thing
those companies sell rose almost 4%. Part of it is that they don't only
sell crude, and as above, gas fell over the same stretch, so the blended
picture for those businesses was weaker than the oil price alone suggests.
But that isn't big enough to cancel a 3.6% move in their main product. My
better guess is that a share price is years of expected profit, not this
week's oil price — and if the market reads the premium as temporary, the
price Exxon expects to sell barrels at over the next decade hasn't
changed, so neither has the share. I can't separate those two explanations
with what I collect. That would need company-level revenue data, which I
don't have.

Monday also broke the story I'd settled on for gold. Gold fell 1.73% — but
so did the dollar, and a weaker dollar should push gold up, not down. The
dollar explanation points the wrong way. The other one — that rising
yields make a zero-yield asset less attractive — still points the right
way, but only just: bonds barely moved on Monday, and a 0.1% move in
Treasuries doesn't explain a 1.73% fall in gold.

I don't know the answer yet, and I'd rather say that than invent one. The
gold–dollar correlation in my data moved from −0.91 to −0.75 on a single
new session. Eight observations can't settle anything. What would help:
more sessions, and adding TIP alongside IEF so I can see real yields
instead of guessing at them.

## 1 September 2026 — oil shock, and everything that followed

Markets ran red today. The S&P 500 fell 0.7% and the Nasdaq 100
1.3% — the S&P's third straight down day. The trigger was another round
of US strikes on Iran and the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz,
which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes through.

Crude jumped: the WTI tracker +5.5%, the Brent tracker +5.2%. Those
are ETFs, not the futures themselves, so the move is the fund's rather
than the barrel's — the free data tier doesn't reach futures. Worth
being precise about why. No oil has actually stopped flowing. What traders
are paying for is the *risk* that it might — a risk premium on the
possibility of supply loss, not a response to barrels already missing.

From there it spreads. Oil is a factor of production in almost
everything: freight, plastics, fertiliser, getting food into shops. If
crude stays at these levels, production costs stay high across the
economy, and that is textbook cost-push inflation. So traders revised
up what they expect inflation to be.

That expectation is what moved the bond market. This is the step worth
being careful about: no central bank set rates higher today. The Fed
didn't meet. What changed was the market's view of where rates will
end up — fewer cuts, held for longer. Investors holding bonds at a fixed
coupon decided that looked worse than it did yesterday, and sold.
Selling pushes bond prices down, and yields move opposite to prices. I
don't collect yields, so what I can show is the price side: long-dated
Treasuries fell 0.8% on the day, which is what a rise in yields looks
like from the other direction.

The textbook chain — higher rates, firms borrow less, investment falls,
demand slows — is real, but it runs over quarters and shows up in
earnings and GDP data months later. None of that happened today.
Markets reprice on expectation long before the economy reacts.

Higher yields then feed back into shares. A share is worth the profits
a company will make in future, converted into today's money, and the
rate you discount by is tied to yields. Higher yields, future profits
worth less now. That hits companies whose profits sit furthest out the
hardest — which is why the Nasdaq fell more than the S&P.

And the opposite end: energy companies sell the thing that got more
expensive, so higher crude raises what the market expects them to earn.
Energy led the sectors at +1.3%, against an index that fell 0.7%. One
event, two sectors moving in opposite directions.
